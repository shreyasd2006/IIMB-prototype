# CPP Conference on Public Policy & Management 2026 concludes after three days of engaging discussions, knowledge sharing and meaningful interactions
{IMG /sites/default/files/2026-09/Panel%20Discussion%20-%20JJM%201.jpg}
P olicymakers, academicians and researchers discuss how sustained and strategic commitment can help India in its path to self-reliance and becoming a developed nation
4 September, 2026, Bengaluru: The concluding day of the XXI International Conference on Public Policy & Management, hosted by the [https://www.iimb.ac.in/centres-of-excellence/cpp] Centre for Public Policy (CPP) from 2nd to 4th September 2026 at IIM Bangalore, featured key sessions showcasing some of the latest developments in policy research and practice. The theme for the 2026 CPP Conference was ‘ Atmanirbhar Bharat ’. The three-day conference saw p olicymakers, academicians and researchers evaluating India’s public programs, their successes and shortfalls, drawing out a roadmap for the future. During the talks, many of the experts highlighted the role institutions such as IIMB and its CPP can play to link research to social development, charting out a government agenda. A key takeaway from all the talks was that attaining Viksit Bharat status by 2047 cannot be a government agenda alone – the process needs to be owned by all stakeholders.
CPP has been organizing the international conference on public policy and management every year since 2005. This year, the conference received 490 research paper submissions, out of which 64 were selected for full presentation, and 56 for poster presentations. For more details, please visit: [https://www.iimb.ac.in/cpp-xxi-2026/] https://www.iimb.ac.in/cpp-xxi-2026/
Panel discussions
Day 3 of the conference opened with two panel discussions.
The first panel discussion on, ‘Bridging the Skill Gap: Aligning Industry, Academia and Government for a Future-Ready Workforce’ was moderated by Prof. [https://www.iimb.ac.in/user/56/sankarshan-basu] Sankarshan Basu of the Finance & Accounting area of IIMB. The panel featured NN Rao, Technical Advisor to the Karnataka Skill Development Authority (KSDA) and University Court Member of Delhi Skill and Entrepreneurship University (DSEU), Dr. B. S. Satyanarayana, Vice Chancellor, Dayananda Sagar University and F. R. Singhvi, Joint Managing Director, Sansera Engineering Ltd.
NN Rao drew attention to the mismatch between candidates’ quality or qualifications and demands from the industry. “We have noticed that more than 90% of the candidates lack the required job skills. Even life skills – the ability to coordinate and communicate, are missing. There are also issues related to geographical location, wage gap, competency and technical expertise, and more. Schools are not able to teach basic values. The lack of trust between academia and industry is another major roadblock.”
He went on to discuss various interventions by the Karnataka Government to mitigate such challenges – related to skill development, solving societal problems, ramping up start-up scaling, industry linkage to training initiatives and boosting sustainable employment. “But the disconnect between academia, government and industry needs to be dealt with through concrete steps – they need to collaborate actively for successful implementation of initiatives. A radical change is needed to enhance quality of programs.”
Dr. B. S. Satyanarayana walked the audience through whether students need to be made job ready or career ready. “Education should groom students with knowledge, skills and associated life values. Earlier, the knowledge and skilling life cycles were much longer than what they are currently. We need to remember that the crowning glory of knowledge is effective use of it. What is needed now is immersive, experiential learning, where knowledge and skills are integrated seamlessly to make students future ready. Knowledge and quality of knowledge, skills, value, attitude – and the ability to use all these seamlessly – should be the primary focus.”
Collaboration and cocreation between academia and industry is the key to achieve all this, he added. “We can link all these and bring experiential learning through multidisciplinary approach. Project based learning will bring in great foundational knowledge and the competence of critical thinking and problem solving, which are essential to adapt to the evolving job market. Education should be scaled up in terms of quality and affordability. Instead of compliance, trust should be given focus, since compliance, processes and expectations do not always match.” He also emphasized on the importance of product-process solutions, R&D especially by industry, support to academic institutions, collaborations at multiple levels and vocational training. “Not just profit or profession, but students’ passion should be the key driver.”
F. R. Singhvi observed that since independence, India’s traditional family dynamics often fostered strict hierarchies, and hence, fear and anxiety. He also said that over the years, due to government rules and regulations, a lot of control came into the hands of bureaucrats and politicians, and the economy focused on money - which brought about an erosion of values and nature of the people, which in turn was responsible for some harmful social and business practices to emerge.
“However, in the past few years, a lot of good schemes have been launched by the government; we need more policies towards public welfare. Care should be taken to ensure that the government’s developmental initiatives reach the intended beneficiaries. It is also the responsibility of industry to develop job ready talent, with an increase in spending and training. The focus should be on boosting communication and making the workforce more enterprising instead of fearful. Skills need to be assessed and the value system should be disciplined through an attitude overhaul. I am hopeful that a thriving start-up culture in the country, supported by digital adoption and public policies, will contribute to the overall development of the country. For this to happen, all the stakeholders must work together by building trust.”
Summing up the discussion, Prof. Sankarshan Basu shared that the Government had set up a taskforce in 2024 to look at around 13 verticals for education and skilling and how that would impact the workforce. “It is set to be revived now, with skill gap analysis as well as training of people to bridge that gap being part of the Government’s agenda”, he added.
The second panel discussion on, ‘Jal Jeevan Mission: From Pipeline to Policy – Ensuring Sustainability Beyond Har Ghar Jal’ was moderated by Prof. [https://www.iimb.ac.in/centres-of-excellence/cpp/senior-fellow] Gopal Naik , Chairperson, Senior Fellow and Jal Jeevan Mission Chair Professor at the Centre for Public Policy, IIM Bangalore. The discussion brought together perspectives from Shri Parameswaran Iyer, former Executive Director, World Bank and former Secretary, Department of Drinking Water & Sanitation (DDWS); Liby T Johnson, Executive Director, Gram Vikas; Fouzia Taranum, Commissioner, RDWSD, Government of Karnataka; Anuj Sharma, Chief Executive Officer, Arghyam, Bengaluru, and Jafar Shareef Sutar, Deputy Secretary (Admin.), Rural Drinking Water and Sanitation Department (RDWSD), Government of Karnataka.
The discussion showcased experiences from the policy, government, civil society and technology areas. Prof. Gopal Naik opened the discussion by framing sustainability across four dimensions – source, institutional, financial and operational sustainability – emphasizing the need to look beyond asset creation to the long-term functioning of water-supply systems.
Shri Parameswaran Iyer drew on experiences from community-based approaches and rural utility models to highlight the importance of moving towards professionalized service delivery. “Trained personnel, appropriate institutional models and mechanisms for recovering O&M costs are critical to long-term functionality”, he added.
Fouzia Taranum shared Karnataka’s experiences in community ownership, water budgeting, water quality monitoring, volumetric billing and local O&M mechanisms, demonstrating how institutional and operational practices can improve sustainability.
Liby T Johnson brought a grounded perspective on the realities of community management, cautioning against assuming that communities will automatically take on the responsibility for managing water systems. “Rather than romanticizing community ownership, focus should be on the need to understand users, incentives and the economics of water services”, he suggested.
Anuj Sharma focused his talk on the role of technology in strengthening governance, monitoring and trust, while emphasizing that technology must be designed to empower communities, rather than to centralize decision-making.
Jafar Shareef Sutar highlighted Karnataka’s efforts towards asset management and predictive maintenance, including the mapping and digitization of water-supply assets. “Knowing what assets exist, where they are located and what maintenance they require, is an important first step towards moving from reactive repairs to more systematic management”, he observed.
The Q&A session further brought out an important observation: there is no single model of sustainable rural water management. When asked how different perspectives can be reconciled, Shri Parameswaran Iyer pointed towards managing water services at the lowest appropriate level, while Fouzia Taranum said that organizations and governments need to ensure that households have reliable access and know where to turn when something goes wrong.
The discussion highlighted the importance of professionalizing service delivery, developing locally relevant models for operations, and using technology to build trust within communities.
The discussion ended with a vote of thanks and felicitation of the panelists by Prof. Gopal Naik.
Closing session
Prof. Gopal Naik in his closing remarks said that the CPP conference on public policy and management is aimed to support PhD students to pursue research on public policy and present their research, as well as to motivate them to do more rigorous work on public policy and make the same available to practitioners. “This interaction between researchers and practitioners will be the key to identification of more areas for research. Practitioners will also get to know the results of studies already conducted.”
Discussing the road ahead, Prof. Naik spoke about plans for setting up an association for researchers and practitioners, which will conduct in-depth group conferences.
Shri Parameswaran Iyer, during the valedictory address, congratulated CPP on its great breadth of work and conducting the conference which featured discussions spanning almost every major dimension of India’s developmental journey. “Some of the issues discussed here will become important research topics, as CPP continues to impact academia, research, policy making and implementation.”
Observing that the pathway to Viksit Bharat is through making India globally competitive, he added, “It is not just important to attain self-reliance, but also the resilience to handle shocks, move forward aggressively and get integrated into Global Value Chains. One clear goal is to score well in the United Nations’ Sustainable Development Goals (SDG) index by 2030. People’s aspirations have increased and that needs to be addressed. Public policy should focus on challenges and providing solutions for the same. The impact of policy on people’s lives is of paramount importance.” Elaborating on the importance of implementation itself, he recommended that to implement programs and transform people's lives, it was important to align, believe, communicate, democratize, evaluate and follow through.
The conference concluded with the vote of thanks delivered by Dr. Anjana Dube, who emphasized the importance of public policy as a research idea and an implementation tool.
